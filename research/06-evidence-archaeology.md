# 06 - Physical and archaeological evidence: what the primary record says

Research date: 2026-10-09/10. Scope: the physical and archaeological evidence that Geoffrey Drumm's "Land of Chem" sulfuric-acid hypothesis cites, and the mainstream evidence it has to account for. Chemistry kinetics, fluid mechanics, electromagnetics and acoustics are covered by other domain files; here they are only touched where they decide how an observation should be read.

Conventions used below
- "verified = yes" means I opened the cited primary source (or a full-text reproduction of it) in this session and read the figure there. "yes (abstract)" means I read it in the paper's own abstract, not the full text. "no (secondary page)" means I did read the figure this session, but only on a secondary page (for example Wikipedia) that cites a primary source I could not open; treat it as unverified. Plain "no" means the figure came from a search-result snippet or a page that refused to load. Everything marked "no" must be re-checked before use.

- Source numbers in square brackets refer to the Sources section.

## Summary

1. **The salt analysis is real but thin, and it is not diagnostic.** Christopher Dunn's book reports that in 1978 Patrick Flanagan had a Queen's Chamber salt sample examined by the Arizona Bureau of Geology and Mineral Technology, which found a mixture of calcium carbonate, sodium chloride and calcium sulfate (gypsum) [4]. No original lab report, method, sample location or quantities could be found. Every one of those three phases is already expected in the chamber without any acid: the walls are limestone (CaCO3); the pyramid's mortar is gypsum plaster in which anhydrite is still converting to gypsum and from which halite exudes under humidity [8][9][10]; and halite and gypsum occur naturally in the Giza limestone [11]. So the observation has roughly the same probability under "acid plant" and under "ordinary gypsum-mortared limestone building in a humid interior". It cannot be called direct evidence of sulfuric acid.
   *[Verification 2026-10-10: a primary record does exist in reproduction. Noone's book prints the Bureau's letter of 27 November 1978 (sample no. 56411, signed R. T. O'Hair, Associate Mineralogist); it ranks the sample as mostly calcite with some halite and gypsum [43]. A 2026 ScanPyramids study found the present Queen's Chamber efflorescence to be halite, with XRD showing only halite, calcite and quartz and no gypsum crust [44]. See "Verification pass" at the end of this file.]*
2. **"Calcium sulfate coats the Grand Gallery walls" has no traceable analysis.** The Dunn passage is about the Queen's Chamber only [4]. Petrie (1883) records salt in the Queen's Chamber and its niche and none in the Grand Gallery [5]. A thick salt crust was reportedly cleaned from the Gallery in the 1990s (about 5 cm, unverified press report), but no composition was published that I could find.
   *[Verification 2026-10-10: three corrections. (i) Dunn's book does assert, without a source, that salt occurs along the Horizontal Passage and in the lower part of the Grand Gallery [46]; only his 1978 analysis is limited to the Queen's Chamber. (ii) Petrie does not describe the Gallery wall surfaces at all, so he cannot be cited for absence. (iii) Hawass (2003) states that salt was cleaned from the Gallery walls during 1998 [45], so a Gallery crust is documented; the Edgars (1910) recorded none there [6]. No composition has been published.]*
3. **Several structural facts the speaker cites are correct**, though the interpretation is his: the Queen's Chamber shafts were left closed at the chamber end by about 5 in of uncut wall stone [5][6]; the southern King's Chamber shaft did end in a short horizontal run at the outer face (Perring 1839, via [7]); no human remains have been documented in the Great Pyramid [30]; both lower-shaft blocking slabs carry two metal pins [17][22].
4. **Several material identifications are visual inferences, not analyses.** The "Gantenbrink door" is called Tura limestone because of its colour and finish; the pins are called copper because they are green [17][18][20]. Neither has been assayed. The claim that Tura limestone is quartz-rich has no source.
5. **One citation is misattributed.** The Dream Stele of Thutmose IV contains no lightning [35]. The lightning-struck sycamore near the Sphinx is in the Inventory Stela, a 26th-Dynasty text [36].
   *[Verification 2026-10-10: softened. The transcript has the speaker say "Sphinx stela" while describing an article by Schoch (full episode, 01:29:03-01:29:46); the words "Dream Stele" are this file's reading, not his. Schoch's 2018 paper is about the Inventory Stela [36], so the citation is ambiguous rather than wrong. The content is still overstated: the text has one sycamore struck near the Sphinx, read from a damaged part of the stone [47]. See claim 13.]*
6. **Three claims have no traceable source at all**: sulfate versus chloride chemistry of the boat pits, a Great Pyramid to Sphinx tunnel confirmed by an unnamed official, and wind "whistle" formations in the Western Field.
7. **The tomb/Khufu attribution rests on independent lines of evidence** that any alternative has to absorb: the Merer logbooks (stone deliveries to "Akhet-Khufu" in the year after Khufu's 13th census) [28], work-gang graffiti naming Khufu in sealed spaces [24], a granite coffer with lid fittings [5], a 4th-Dynasty workers' town [29], sealed boat burials carrying Khufu's and Djedefre's names [24][33], and human remains in other Old Kingdom pyramids [30]. The hypothesis does not say how these fit.
8. **Status of exploration as of 2026-10-10:** Zahi Hawass said in November 2025 that a 30 m corridor would be unveiled in 2026 [41]. Posts attributed to Matt Beall (not openable, unverified) describe repeated slips, with the Big Void attempt most recently targeted for November 2026. I found no report that the Big Void or the space behind the Queen's Chamber north slab has been entered or imaged.
   *[Verification 2026-10-10: unchanged. Still no reported entry or imaging. The exploration team's podcast listing dated 28 August 2026 says new radar data are being analysed and that some findings cannot yet be made public [50]; no ministry statement was found.]*

## Claim-by-claim assessment

Each entry separates (a) what was said, (b) what the primary record shows, (c) the verdict. Verdict vocabulary: supported; partly supported; no evidence; contradicted; untestable; undefined by hypothesis.

### 1. "A 1978 chemical analysis of the Queen's Chamber salt (in Dunn's book) found calcium carbonate, sodium chloride and calcium sulfate"
- Said: clip 2, 18:16-18:59 [2].
- Record: Dunn's text does say this, naming Patrick Flanagan and the Arizona Bureau of Geology and Mineral Technology [4]. It gives no proportions, no method, no sampling point. I could not find the Bureau's report or any independent publication of it. Dunn himself offered a different reading of the same result (hot hydrogen acting on limestone, per the passage as summarised by the search tool), which shows that the data do not select a mechanism.
- Verdict: **supported as a report of what the book says; the underlying analysis is unverified at source.**
- Verification 2026-10-10: the source is now traced one step further. Noone's book (which Dunn cites) reproduces as its Plate 4:22 a letter dated 27 November 1978 from the Bureau's Mineral Technology Branch at the University of Arizona, sample no. 56411, signed by Robert T. O'Hair, Associate Mineralogist, and addressed to "Source of Innergy" in Tucson. It says the sample is mostly calcite with some halite and gypsum, and names no method and no proportions. A separate certification signed by G. Patrick Flanagan says the material was a crystalline deposit from the Queen's Chamber walls [43]. Who took the sample, when, and from what height is not stated. Dunn's list of three compounds is therefore accurate but drops the ranking: calcite is the main phase, the two salts are minor. Dunn's hot-hydrogen reading of the result is confirmed in a scan of his book [46]. The Bureau's own file copy was not looked for beyond the web.
- Revised verdict: **supported.** A laboratory letter exists in published reproduction; it is qualitative, undated as to sampling, and without method. (was: "supported as a report of what the book says; the underlying analysis is unverified at source.")

### 2. "Calcium sulfate is direct evidence of dilute sulfuric acid"
- Record, in favour: gypsum is indeed what forms when sulfuric acid meets calcite, and a gypsum rind does slow further attack. Petrie's note that the niche carries salt on its two lowest laps and none on the upper three [5] is the kind of height-limited pattern a liquid fill line would leave, so it deserves measurement.
- Record, against uniqueness: (i) the pyramid's own mortar is gypsum plaster, with anhydrite still hydrating to gypsum and halite exuding because of interior humidity [8]; XRD on Great Pyramid mortar finds gypsum, calcite and silica [9]; thermal analysis and XRD find gypsum, anhydrite, calcite [10]. (ii) Halite and gypsum are native to the Giza limestone [11], and salt crystallisation driven by moisture is the standard explanation for decay at the Sphinx [11][12]. (iii) The same salt problem is reported in Menkaure's and Khafre's pyramids [15][16], so salt crusts are not specific to the Great Pyramid. (iv) A height-limited salt band is also what capillary rise or a humidity gradient produces. (v) The speaker names anhydrite, shown in a photograph from the Bent Pyramid, as the visual match; aqueous attack at ambient temperature should give the dihydrate, gypsum (general chemistry, not sourced here; see the chemistry file), while anhydrite in these buildings is documented as a mortar phase [8][10].
- Verdict: **partly supported as chemistry, contradicted as "direct evidence".** The observation is expected under both models. It becomes evidence only if a discriminating measurement is made (see Open questions: sulfur isotopes, replacement textures, crust mapping against a fill level).
- Verification 2026-10-10, new evidence against: an independent laboratory study now exists. Sessa et al. (2026, ScanPyramids) took nine micro-samples in the Queen's Chamber and the Thieves' Corridor behind the niche and ran in-situ XRF at 32 spots. The white efflorescence is halite (only Na and Cl by EDX, R = 0.969); XRD on seven samples found only halite, calcite and quartz; the authors report "no clear evidence of widespread gypsum formation and/or crystallization" and no gypsum-rich crust. Efflorescence on the west wall and ceiling grew visibly between 2022 and 2024 (read by one extraction route only), which shows a present-day process. They attribute the halite to the limestone itself, following Klemm and Klemm [44].
- Verification 2026-10-10, what remains in favour: (i) the 1978 letter did find some gypsum [43]; (ii) Sessa et al. measured sulfur by EDX at about 3 % in detached stone and about 6 % in a brown deposit, and could not identify the phase, noting that sulfate phases may lie below the XRD detection limit [44]. If those figures are weight per cent and all the sulfur were gypsum, 3 % S would be about 16 % gypsum by mass (3 / 0.186; my arithmetic, conditional on both assumptions), which XRD would normally see; the paper does not resolve this. (iii) The chamber was cleaned in 1995-1997 without surviving documentation [44], so today's surface need not match the pre-cleaning crust. (iv) Sampling was restricted to efflorescence and loose material; no section through the wall surface exists. None of this is positive evidence for acid; it marks where a test could still succeed.
- Verification 2026-10-10, logic check on the niche datum: in the hypothesis the Queen's Chamber lies below the Grand Gallery and is filled completely, so a fill line part-way up the niche is not what the hypothesis itself predicts. Petrie's two-laps observation is better read as a moisture or capillary limit, and it should not be counted in the hypothesis's favour without a stated partial-fill level.
- Revised verdict: **partly supported as chemistry; contradicted as "direct evidence"; and the premise is weaker than the first pass assumed.** The one modern mineralogical study finds a halite efflorescence and no gypsum coating in the Queen's Chamber. A minor, unidentified sulfur phase remains, and no isotope or micro-textural test has been made.

### 3. "Calcium sulfate was discovered all over the walls of the Grand Gallery"
- Said: clip 2, 18:57-19:12 (prompted by the host) [2].
- Record: not in the Dunn passage [4]. Petrie reports no salt in the Gallery [5]. A press report (unverified) says about 5 cm of crust was removed from the Gallery walls in the 1990s and blamed visitor humidity; no composition given.
- Verdict: **no evidence** (no traceable analysis of Grand Gallery crust).
- Verification 2026-10-10: the first-pass record line needs three corrections. Dunn's book does say, without a source, that salt occurs along the Horizontal Passage and in the lower part of the Grand Gallery [46], so the host's recollection of Dunn is fair; Dunn reports no analysis of it. Petrie does not describe the Gallery wall surfaces, so "Petrie reports no salt in the Gallery" should read "Petrie is silent". Hawass, writing in 2003, says his team spent twelve months in 1998 cleaning salt and graffiti from the Gallery walls, and blames visitors' breath and sweat [45]; the statement that the Gallery was found encrusted in 1993 and the 5 cm figure remain snippet-only.
- Timing evidence: the Edgars, in a passage-by-passage description published in 1910, say the incrustation is found only in the Horizontal Passage and Queen's Chamber, allow a little in the First Ascending Passage, and do not mention the Gallery [6]. A Gallery crust that is absent from that description and present by the 1990s points to growth in the tourism era, not to an ancient process. This is an argument from an omission in one source, so it is suggestive, not conclusive.
- Revised verdict: **partly supported: a salt crust on the Gallery walls is documented for the 1990s; that it was calcium sulfate has no evidence; the timing counts against an ancient origin.** (was: "no evidence")

### 4. "Small pits all over the Queen's Chamber walls are acid attack where the sulfate layer chipped off"
- Record: I found no published description or survey of pitting in the Queen's Chamber. Petrie and the Edgars describe salt incrustation and very fine joints, not pits [5][6]. Pitting, flaking and granular loss are the normal result of salt crystallisation in porous limestone [11][12][14].
- Verdict: **no evidence** for the observation in the literature; the acid reading is the speaker's interpretation of something he reports seeing. Testable by micro-morphology: acid etching leaves dissolution features under a replacement gypsum rind; salt pitting leaves mechanically spalled grains with subflorescence.

### 5. "No body was found, so it was not a tomb"
- Record, fact: no verified human remains from the Great Pyramid; the granite coffer was found open and empty [30][24].
- Record, context: the coffer has an undercut lid groove and three pin-holes for locking a lid [5] and is too large to have been brought in after roofing [24]. Human remains or canopic material have been recovered from the pyramids of Neferefre, Djedkare, Unas, Teti, Pepi I and Merenre, and fragments from the Red Pyramid [30]. The pyramid is thought to have been entered and robbed in antiquity [24]. Absence of a body in a robbed tomb is the expected outcome.
- Verdict: **the fact is supported; the inference is not.** Strictly, "tomb" and "plant" are not mutually exclusive, but the hypothesis asserts non-tomb and gives no account of the funerary evidence in claim 16.

### 6. "The Queen's Chamber shafts were sealed from the chamber by several inches of stone"
- Record: Petrie: the mouths were "covered over the mouth by a plate of stone" left uncut in the wall [5]; the Edgars, after Piazzi Smyth, give about five inches and note it is part of the block, not an inserted plug [6]. Opened by Waynman Dixon in 1872 [24][23].
- Verdict: **supported.** For modelling this is a hard no-flow boundary of about 127 mm limestone at each shaft mouth.

### 7. "The Gantenbrink door is Tura limestone with copper pins; Tura limestone has much higher quartz content"
- Record: the Djedi team describes the last U-block and the slab as finely polished and probably Tura stone, and judges the pins to be copper or mainly copper from their green colour [17]. Gantenbrink's 1993 log calls the last block a lighter limestone like that used for casing, and the dark streaks "copper fittings" on closer inspection [20]. No sample of slab or pin has been analysed. The rear of each pin ends in a small loop; the team thinks they were ornamental [17]. Both the south and north slabs have pins [17][22]. I found no source for elevated quartz in Tura limestone; Tallet describes Tura stone as the fine "white stone" used for casing [28].
- Verdict: **partly supported** (material identifications are plausible visual inferences, not measurements); **quartz claim: no evidence.** Note that the speaker's prediction that metal parts will be found in the north shaft is already met by the pins seen on the north slab in 2002, so it does not discriminate unless it is narrowed to metal behind the slab.

### 8. "The King's Chamber shafts end horizontally and do not target stars" (credited to Matt Sibson)
- Record: Elizabeth Thomas, citing Perring's 1839 plates and Chatley, states that the outer end of the southern channel "turned abruptly to run parallel with the ground" for roughly its last 10 ft, and that both channels leave the chamber horizontally for about 5 ft; Sirius could never have been sighted through it [7]. Petrie adds that the south shaft curves sideways by more than its own width [5]. The outer casing is gone, so the original mouth cannot be inspected; whether the shafts pierced the casing is unknown [25]. I could not locate the Sibson video.
- Interpretation: line-of-sight star observation was ruled out long ago for exactly these reasons [7]. The Egyptological "star shaft" idea concerns the direction of the mean slope as a symbolic route, which a short horizontal outlet does not address either way. A horizontal outlet is equally consistent with keeping sand or rain out (Perring's own suggestion [7]) and with ventilation.
- Verdict: **the geometric fact is supported for the south shaft (presumed for the north); "simply air intakes" is interpretation.** For modelling: outlet is a horizontal duct about 3 m long, section about 0.22 x 0.22 m.

### 9. "The Great Pyramid never had a capstone"
- Record: about 8 m is missing from the top, and classical writers already describe a platform [24]. No fragment of a Great Pyramid pyramidion is documented. Pyramidia are documented for Khufu's own satellite pyramid G1-d (found 1993) and Sneferu's Red Pyramid [31]. The Merer logbooks show casing stone still being delivered in Khufu's last attested year [28], so completion status at his death is not independently fixed.
- Verdict: **no evidence either way for the Great Pyramid itself**; practice at contemporary pyramids favours a pyramidion. The speaker's argument is drawn from a simulation plot, not from archaeology.

### 10. "The boat pits are circuit breakers; sulfate was found in pits by the Great Pyramid and chloride in pits by Khafre's"
- Record: the first southern pit (opened 26 May 1954) was closed by 41 multi-tonne limestone blocks whose joints were filled with hard gypsum plaster, found intact, over a dismantled cedar boat of 1,224 pieces [33][34]. The second southern pit held a second dismantled boat; its air matched outside air, and its cover stones carry painted names of Khufu and Djedefre [34][24]. Three further boat-shaped pits lie east of the pyramid [24]. I found no published chemical analysis of pit walls or floors for either pyramid. Sulfate near the Khufu pits has an obvious documented source (the gypsum plaster), and chloride in rock-cut pits has one too (halite in the bedrock [11]).
- Verdict: **chemistry claim: no evidence (no traceable source). "Circuit breaker" function: contradicted for the two southern pits**, which were permanently sealed over wooden boats and could not be filled and drained; **untested** for the open eastern pits.
- Verification 2026-10-10: the sealing facts were re-read. Jenkins (1980) gives 41 blocks of more than 15 t each, joints chinked with hard gypsum plaster found untouched, 1,224 timber pieces in 13 layers, shelf reached on 26 May 1954, Kamal el-Mallakh leading, and a rebuilt length of 142 ft (43.3 m, my conversion) [33]; the Waseda page gives 41 blocks averaging about 14 t and says air from the second pit was almost the same as outside air [34]. Both are secondary accounts; the excavation report (Nour et al. 1960) was not opened. No chemistry of any pit was found.
- Revised verdict on function: "contradicted" is slightly too strong as logic. The sealing shows the two southern pits ended as boat burials closed under Djedefre; it does not by itself exclude an earlier use. The fair statement is: **no evidence for the chemistry claim; the switch function is inconsistent with the as-found state of the southern pits and has no positive evidence anywhere; a "used before the boats were buried" variant is untested and not specified by the hypothesis.** (was: "contradicted for the two southern pits")

### 11. "Sodium chloride coats Khafre's burial chamber"
- Record: salt residue was cleaned from the interior walls during the 2003-2004 restoration; the antiquities council blamed visitor humidity [16]. The chamber is cut in bedrock [32], and halite is native to that bedrock [11]. No compositional analysis of the Khafre chamber crust was found.
- Verdict: **partly supported** (salt is there; NaCl is plausible but unpublished); as evidence for an HCl process it is **not diagnostic**.

### 12. "An official confirmed a tunnel from the Great Pyramid to the Sphinx enclosure"

## What a simulation of this domain needs

This domain supplies constraints and discriminating tests more than governing physics, but two forward models are needed so that the evidence can count for or against.

### Model A: acid-on-limestone wall reaction (the hypothesis)
- Governing equations: transport of H+ and SO4(2-) through a growing gypsum product layer on calcite (shrinking-core form): flux J = D_eff (C_bulk - C_interface) / delta(t), with d(delta)/dt = J * V_m(gypsum) / (1 - porosity), coupled to bulk liquid mass balance and to CO2 release. Dissolution and re-precipitation of gypsum in the bulk (solubility of order 2 g/L, quoted from memory and unverified; take the value from the chemistry file) sets whether a rind persists or sloughs.
- Boundary and initial conditions: wetted area and fill height history from the hydraulics file; acid concentration and temperature history from the chemistry file; wall mineralogy = fine limestone with gypsum-plaster joints; Queen's Chamber shaft mouths closed by 127 mm stone (no-flow); floor of the Queen's Chamber is rough core masonry [5] (leak path to be modelled, not assumed sealed).
- Dimensionless numbers: Damkohler number (surface reaction rate over diffusive supply through the rind); Biot-type mass-transfer number (liquid film versus rind resistance); Sherwood number for the slowly rising free surface.
- Output to compare with evidence: gypsum mass per m2 as a function of height and number of cycles; position of the upper edge of the crust; acid consumed per cycle by walls and joints; integrity of gypsum-plaster joints after N cycles.

### Model B: salt efflorescence without acid (the null)
- Governing equations: coupled heat, moisture and ion transport in porous limestone and gypsum mortar (Richards-type unsaturated flow plus vapour diffusion; advection-diffusion of Na+, Cl-, Ca2+, SO4(2-); phase equilibria for halite and gypsum/anhydrite via a Pitzer-type model).
- Boundary and initial conditions: interior air temperature and RH time series (pre-tourism closed state versus 85 % RH visitor state, the latter unverified; measured replacement values added 2026-10-10: 75.3 % RH at 31.4 C after a night without visitors and 89.7 % RH at 32 C with visitors [44], which straddles the halite deliquescence point of 75.2 % at that temperature; these readings are from the paper's King's Chamber section, so daily dissolve-and-recrystallise cycling of Queen's Chamber wall salt is an inference to be tested, not a measurement); initial salt inventory from the limestone [11] and mortar [8]; evaporation at wall faces; no liquid fill.
- Key thresholds and numbers: halite deliquescence 75.4 % RH at 20 C [13], lower in mixtures (about 65 % [14]); Peclet number for salt advection to the evaporation front versus back-diffusion (Pe > 1 gives surface crust, Pe < 1 gives distributed salt); capillary rise height.
- Output: predicted crust phases, thickness and vertical distribution after centuries.

### What pass/fail would look like
The hypothesis **passes** this domain only if Model A reproduces something Model B cannot, and that thing is then found. Candidates:
1. A crust whose upper limit follows a level surface at the modelled fill height in both the Queen's Chamber and the Grand Gallery (Model B predicts a limit tied to capillary rise and air circulation, not a common datum).
2. Gypsum as a replacement rind on etched calcite, with dissolution textures beneath it, rather than efflorescence on an intact tooled surface.
3. Sulfur isotope composition of crust gypsum distinct from the mortar gypsum and from bedrock gypsum.
4. Systematic absence of NaCl in the wetted zone (dilute acid would flush chloride) with NaCl only above the fill line.
5. Acid-leached, widened joints in the wetted zone.

The hypothesis **fails** this domain if: the crust is compositionally and isotopically the same as the mortar and bedrock salts; its distribution follows moisture pathways rather than a fill level; original tool marks and tight plastered joints survive in the supposedly wetted zone; or Model A predicts crust thicknesses and joint damage far outside what is observed for any plausible number of cycles.

Evidence that cannot be simulated and must be handled as priors or documented constraints: textual attribution (claims 13, 16), the unnamed-official tunnel (12), and the Western Field "whistles" (14).

### Geometry and boundary facts this file hands to other domains
- Queen's Chamber shafts: closed at the chamber by about 127 mm of wall stone until 1872; closed again at 63.6 +/- 0.4 m by a slab about 60 mm thick with a 190 x 230 x 230 mm cavity and a second, rough block behind [17]. They are dead-end ducts, not flow paths.
- King's Chamber shafts: about 0.22 x 0.22 m, horizontal for about 1.5 m at the chamber and about 3 m at the outer face [7], mean slopes about 31 and 44.5-45 degrees [5], laterally curved; open to the exterior today, original casing condition unknown [25].
- Big Void: at least 30 m long above the Grand Gallery, section and inclination unresolved [37]; it should be a free parameter with wide priors, and no inlet or outlet may be assumed.
- Southern boat pits: sealed volumes containing timber; not available as open electrolyte basins.

### Steelman: the best published support for the hypothesis in this domain

1. Gypsum is really there. A state laboratory reported some gypsum in a Queen's Chamber wall deposit in 1978 [43], and the 2026 study measured sulfur at the per-cent level in loose stone and in a brown deposit at the chamber entrance, without identifying the phase [44].
2. The record of the surface is damaged. The chamber was cleaned and its cavities filled in 1995-1997 with no surviving documentation [44]; a crust present before then could have been removed. The 2026 sampling was limited to efflorescence and loose fragments, so a thin sulfate layer bonded to the stone has not been excluded.
3. Salt is concentrated exactly where the hypothesis puts liquid: the Queen's Chamber and Horizontal Passage, with little elsewhere in 1910 [6]. A null model has to explain that distribution too (candidates: these are the spaces lined in fine limestone and closest to the core mortar, with the least air exchange; this is untested).
4. A lightning event at the Sphinx is recorded in an Egyptian text, whatever its date [47].
5. Nothing published tests the discriminating predictions (sulfur isotopes, replacement textures, joint leaching). The hypothesis is therefore untested on its own best ground, not refuted on it.

Against that, the strongest published evidence the other way is also new: the visible salt is halite, not calcium sulfate; it grew between 2022 and 2024 on the west wall and the ceiling (this detail was read by one extraction route only); the one pair of humidity readings in the paper, which is from its King's Chamber section, crosses the halite deliquescence point between night and day [44]; the mortar is a gypsum plaster that sheds halite [8][48]; and a Gallery crust is first recorded in the era of mass tourism [6][45]. Point 3 of the steelman also cuts both ways: the hypothesis fills the Grand Gallery as well, and the 1910 description has no salt there.
