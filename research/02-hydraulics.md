# 02 - Hydraulics (water domain): evidence and simulation inputs

Scope: the water-handling part of Geoffrey Drumm's "Land of Chem" hypothesis for the Great Pyramid (Khufu), as stated in the three local transcripts (History Drops parts 1 and 2; Danny Jones episode 00:36:30 to 00:56:40, plus 01:14:28 to 01:25:13 and the water-table remark at 02:41:43 to 02:43:42) [S1].
Research date: 2026-10-09/10. All elevations "rel. base" are relative to the pyramid pavement (Petrie's zero); "asl" is above sea level.

How to read the "verified" column: `true` = I read that figure in the cited source in this session. `false` = derived by my own arithmetic from verified inputs, or taken from a secondary page / search snippet / memory (the reason is stated). Petrie's chapter was read through a text-extraction tool; several of his tables were garbled in extraction, so Petrie rows marked `true` are single sentences or the Section 64 summary table that came through cleanly.

## 1. Summary

1. **What the hypothesis says (water part).** An external reservoir surrounds the pyramid and feeds the Subterranean Chamber; one-way valves sit in the chamber's pit; water rises in the well shaft to reservoir level; a pump travelling in the descending passage "compresses" water to fill the Queen's Chamber and Grand Gallery in four activations; retracting the pump draws more water up through the pit from an aquifer "directly below"; draining the Grand Gallery then acts as a syringe, pulling air down the King's Chamber shafts and gas through four grooves in the antechamber; product is "sucked out" through the Queen's Chamber. Drumm cites John Cadman's ram-pump model (but rejects Cadman's output line, because the south passage is a dead end) and an unnamed "German engineering team" drawing with a one-way valve [S1].

2. **Elevations decide most of it.** The pyramid base is at about 60 m asl [S5]. Lehner's reconstruction of the 4th Dynasty waterfront puts the flood peak at 13.5 to 14.0 m asl, the floodplain at 12 m asl and low water at 7 m asl, with Khufu's valley temple platform at 14.5 m asl about 400 m from the plateau edge [S3]. Nile-fed water therefore stood roughly 46 m below the pyramid base at its annual maximum. The Sheisha 2022 and Ghoneim 2024 papers support navigable water at the *foot* of the plateau; neither claims water on the plateau [S6][S7]. The modern water table is about 15 to 17 m asl, i.e. about 43 to 45 m below the base, and one borehole on 65 m ground found water at 15 m [S4].
   [Verification 2026-10-10: all figures in this item re-read and confirmed. Addition: Younes et al. 2024 [S18] argue the 4th Dynasty harbour levels were about 3 m higher than Lehner's (low water about 10 m, flood about 17 m asl), which puts the flood peak about 43 m below the base instead of 46 m. No value for the water table under Khufu itself exists in [S4].]

3. **The Subterranean Chamber is above every documented water table.** Its floor is about 30 to 32 m below the base (about 28 to 30 m asl) [S2]: roughly 14 to 16 m above the Old Kingdom flood peak and 11 to 15 m above today's water table. The pit bottom (about 36 to 41 m below base, sources disagree) is nearer the modern water table but still above Lehner's Old Kingdom flood level on the best-supported depth.
   [Verification 2026-10-10: corrected. The deep pit is an 1837-38 excavation (Vyse/Perring), 36 to 38 ft through solid rock, not an ancient feature; before 1837 the pit ended about 34.6 m below base (and part of that may be Caviglia's 1817 digging). The 1838 shaft bottom is 41 to 43 m below base (17 to 19 m asl; one reading that Perring's own arithmetic does not support would give 46 m), i.e. about 0 to 4 m above the modern water table and at or just above the top of the Old Kingdom flood-peak range (13.5 to 17 m asl, see the widened row in 2.2). See "Verification pass", checks 5 to 8.]

4. **Water has been seen in the pit, with a mundane cause.** Petrie (1880-82) recorded that the roughly 30 ft pit holds water "after heavy rains have rushed down the entrance passage" [S2]. That is direct evidence of episodic water, and it is evidence for rain inflow, not for an aquifer connection. Drumm's "wet sand, level fluctuates" observation is anecdotal and not found in any publication I could open.

5. **First-principles numbers (section 4).** Filling to the top of the Grand Gallery needs about 0.80 MPa (81.5 m head) at Subterranean Chamber level; the force on a piston filling the descending passage is about 0.58 to 0.95 MN (59 to 97 tonnes-force); the spaces to be filled hold about 860 m3 while one full 105 m stroke of the passage sweeps only about 133 m3, so "four activations" does not close on volume (about 6.5 full-length strokes, about 10 if only the bedrock part below base level is used); ideal work is about 260 MJ from a base-level reservoir, about 640 MJ from the Old Kingdom water table. Suction cannot lift water more than about 10 m, which is less than the 14 to 23 m between the Old Kingdom water table and the chamber floor.
   [Verification 2026-10-10: every number in this item was recomputed independently and reproduced (0.80 MPa; 580 to 950 kN; 860 m3; 133 and 85 m3 per stroke; 6.5 and 10.1 strokes; 260 and 640 MJ). The lift range widens to 11 to 23 m when the higher flood level of [S18] is included.]

6. **Containment is the least constrained input.** Core ("backing") limestone of Khufu's pyramid has 21 to 33 percent total porosity and 12 to 20 percent water absorption; the mortar is gypsum-based (gypsum dissolves at about 2 g/L); casing joints average 0.5 mm [S5][S2][S13]. Drumm's statement that the limestone "is not permeable" is contradicted for the measured backing stone. No measured hydraulic conductivity for Mokattam or Tura stone was found; leak-off is therefore a sensitivity parameter (cubic in joint aperture).

7. **Syringe claim.** Draining a closed vessel does draw gas in (continuity), and the King's Chamber shafts are large enough that the pressure difference needed is tens of pascals. The claim that the gallery's inclination is what makes it work is not supported by physics, and the hypothesis does not say where the drained water goes. Geometry also raises an unaddressed problem: the gallery's exit to the antechamber is at its floor level at the high end, about 8.6 m below the gallery roof, so a full gallery would either trap a gas pocket or push water into the antechamber and King's Chamber.

8. **Cadman.** Small-scale models, reported (second-hand, via Malkowski) to pulse at 60 to 80 per minute; no pressures, flows, lift heights or scale are published, no peer-reviewed test or formal rebuttal was found. A hydraulic ram must discharge most of its drive water below the supply level; a blind chamber 30 m down in bedrock has no such outfall.

Overall: none of the hydraulic claims is supported by archaeological or hydrogeological evidence; several are contradicted by elevation data or by simple volume and suction arithmetic; a few (communicating vessels, syringe displacement) are physically sound in principle but conditional on undefined seals, valves, actuators and a water source for which there is no evidence.

## 3. Claim-by-claim assessment

Verdict vocabulary: supported / partly supported / no evidence / contradicted / untestable / undefined by hypothesis.

### C1. "External reservoir surrounding the Great Pyramid" - contradicted (as a Nile-fed body); no evidence (as any body of water)

- For: Herodotus and Pliny wrote of Nile-fed channels or a well under the pyramid (reported second-hand by [S12]); Petrie saw water-wear in the NNE rock trench and thought it looked like a drain [S2]; rain does reach the plateau; modern perched water exists on high ground to the W and SW [S4]. Navigable Nile water did come to the foot of the plateau [S3][S6][S7].

### C2. "The reservoir was used to introduce water into the Subterranean Chamber" - undefined by hypothesis

The only known connection from outside to the chamber is the descending passage, whose mouth is 16.97 m above the pavement on the north face [S2]. A reservoir at pavement level cannot flow into it; a reservoir 17 m deep around a 230 m base is not proposed and has no evidence. Cadman's model simply assumes the passage is the inlet [S9]. The inlet route, its elevation and its control are not specified.

### C3. "One-way valves in the pit" and the "German engineering team" drawing - no evidence

No valve, seat, hinge socket or fitting is reported by Petrie in the pit or chamber [S2]. The drawing shown in the video could not be identified; German-language searching returned only forum and popular material. Listed under open questions.

### C4. "Water rises in the well shaft to the level of the external reservoir" - partly supported (physics only)

Communicating vessels: true if a reservoir is connected and the conduits hold water. But the well shaft top is at +21.66 m [S2]; with a base-level reservoir the water would stand 21.7 m below the gallery. The shaft also crosses a gravel-filled fissure lined only with courses of small stones (the grotto) [S2], an obvious leak path.

### C5. "A pump in the descending passage compresses water and fills the Queen's Chamber and Grand Gallery" - no evidence; severe quantitative demands

No piston, rail, seal, rope wear or actuator is described in the sources opened. Quantitatively (section 4): 0.46 to 0.75 MPa at the piston face, 0.58 to 0.95 MN force; water is nearly incompressible, so this is displacement, not compression. Petrie measured the passage straight to a mean error of about 0.23 to 0.30 in [S2], which bounds the clearance a sliding piston's seal must accommodate (several millimetres); a 1 mm annular gap at 0.75 MPa would leak on the order of 0.1 m3/s (section 4.6).

### C6. "Four activations of the pump fill the Grand Gallery" - contradicted by geometry (with one narrow exception)

Spaces that must fill before or with the gallery hold about 860 m3; one stroke of the entire 105 m passage sweeps about 133 m3. That is about 6.5 full-length strokes, or about 10 strokes if only the part below base level is used. The gallery alone (about 570 m3) is about 4.3 full-length strokes, so "four" is only reachable if the Queen's Chamber, both passages and the well shaft are somehow already full and the piston travels the entire passage, which leaves no room for the piston itself or an inlet.

[Verification 2026-10-10: arithmetic reproduced independently (working W3). With water standing at base level only the 67 m of passage below base can displace water (85 m3 per stroke, 340 m3 in four). Verdict stands.]

### C7. "Retracting the pump draws water from an aquifer directly below, through the pit" - contradicted

Suction lift is limited to about 10 m of water (section 4.4). Lehner's Old Kingdom water levels (7 to 14 m asl) [S3] are 14 to 23 m below the chamber floor (28 to 30 m asl); the modern table (15 to 17 m asl) [S4] is 11 to 15 m below it. Perring's 1837-38 levels, if the snippet is accurate, agree (42 to 48 m below base) [S15]. A piston higher up the passage has even more lift to overcome. The verdict would change only if a perched aquifer above about 20 m asl existed under Khufu in the Old Kingdom; none is documented there.

### C8. "The pit is filled with wet sand; the water level fluctuates; so there is still water below" - partly supported as an observation, not as an inference

Petrie independently recorded water in the pit after heavy rain ran down the entrance passage [S2], and the Edgars are reported to have put sand and stones back into the pit in 1909 [S12, secondary]. So damp fill is credible. It does not establish an aquifer link: rain inflow is the documented cause, and the modern water table is itself raised by leakage and irrigation [S4].

### C9. "Draining the Grand Gallery acts like a syringe, pulling air through the King's Chamber shafts and gas through the antechamber" - partly supported (physics only)

Continuity guarantees that gas replaces drained water through the lowest-resistance opening. The two shafts (about 0.05 m2 each, about 53 and 71 m) pass the required flow at tens of pascals (section 4.5). Conditions not met or not stated: the gallery and antechamber must be gas-tight everywhere else; the shafts must be open (Petrie found the south one blocked by sand 76 ft down and the north one blocked [S2]); and the hypothesis never says where the drained water goes.

### C10. "The gallery must be inclined for the syringe to work (vertical would not connect, horizontal would not give enough time)" - no evidence; not required by physics

Volume of gas drawn equals volume of water drained regardless of inclination. Inclination changes only the free-surface area and the level-versus-volume curve. A simulation can test whether inclination changes gas-liquid contact time, but nothing in hydraulics requires it.

### C11. "The antechamber was sealed by three granite blocks so only gas passes through four grooves; water cannot enter the King's Chamber" - undefined by hypothesis / internally inconsistent as stated

The gallery's exit is at floor level at the high end (+42.9 to +43.0 m); the gallery roof there is about +51.5 m; the King's Chamber floor is +43.0 m and its roof +48.8 m [S2]. Grooves that pass gas pass water. If the gallery is filled above +43 m, either gas is trapped and compressed in the roof space (the gallery does not fill), or water enters the antechamber and King's Chamber up to the same level, or the King's Chamber gas is held at up to about 84 kPa gauge, which the open shafts would vent. The portcullis slabs are not in place today; their sealing is an assumption.

### C12. "The limestone of the Great Pyramid is not permeable" - contradicted for the measured stone

Backing limestone: 21 to 33 percent total porosity, 12 to 20 percent water absorption; gypsum mortar; karstified, fractured bedrock [S5]. The fine lining stone of the chambers and gallery was not tested in the sources opened, so its permeability is unknown, not zero.

[Verification 2026-10-10: verdict softened to "contradicted as an absolute statement; through-flow permeability unmeasured". What [S5] measured is porosity and water absorption, which show the backing stone takes up water; it reports no permeability. The same paper puts about 96 percent of the pore volume of five Khufu specimens in the 0.02 to 0.23 micrometre class, which is the signature of a low-permeability micro-porous rock. ### C14. "The product is sucked out of the Queen's Chamber" and "the Big Void was a water-filled heat exchanger with a fill shaft" - undefined by hypothesis

No outlet from the Queen's Chamber is specified; its two small channels were left unpierced at the chamber end according to Drumm [S1] (not independently checked in this domain). Drumm labels the Big Void idea speculative himself [S1].

### C15. "The SAR team shows the water table far too deep" (02:41 to 02:43) - partly supported

### What the hypothesis leaves undefined (water domain)

## 4. First-principles calculations (working shown)

Constants: rho = 1000 kg/m3, g = 9.81 m/s2 (rho g = 9.81 kPa per metre), water viscosity 1.0e-3 Pa s, air density 1.2 kg/m3, air kinematic viscosity 1.5e-5 m2/s. These are textbook values, not looked up this session. Passage area A = 1.0541 m x 1.2014 m = 1.266 m2 (rounded to 1.27; 1.20 if 1.0 x 1.2 m is used). Levels from section 2.1.

### 4.1 Hydrostatic pressure

| from Subterranean Chamber passage floor (-30.0 m) to | head (m) | pressure (kPa) |
|---|---|---|
| top of well shaft / foot of gallery (+21.66) | 51.7 | 507 |
| Queen's Chamber ridge (+27.40) | 57.4 | 563 |
| gallery floor, high end (+42.90) | 72.9 | 715 |
| gallery roof, high end (+51.5) | 81.5 | 800 |

So about 0.80 MPa (about 7.9 atm gauge) at chamber level with the gallery brim-full; about 0.82 MPa at the pit rim (-31.9 m).

### 4.2 Force on a piston filling the descending passage

F = rho g (z_surface - z_piston) A.

- Piston at -25 m, water at gallery roof +51.5 m: 9.81 x 76.5 x 1.266 = 950 kN (about 97 tonnes-force).
- Piston at -25 m, water just reaching gallery foot +21.66 m: 9.81 x 46.66 x 1.266 = 580 kN.
- Piston at base level 0 m, water at +51.5 m: 9.81 x 51.5 x 1.266 = 640 kN.

Can a stone plug's own weight do it? Granite (2650 kg/m3 assumed) filling the passage weighs 1.266 x 2650 x 9.81 = 32.9 kN per metre of plug; the down-slope component is 32.9 x sin 26.52 deg (0.4466) = 14.7 kN/m; friction at mu = 0.5 is 0.5 x 32.9 x cos 26.52 deg (0.8948) = 14.7 kN/m, i.e. a stone-on-stone plug does not even slide. With zero friction, 950 kN needs 950 / 14.7 = 65 m of solid granite in a 105 m passage. A gravity plunger is therefore not sufficient; an external actuator of order 1 MN is required.

### 4.3 Volumes, stroke count, work

Volumes (my estimates from Petrie's dimensions):

| space | working | volume (m3) | centroid (m rel. base) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grand Gallery | vertical section about 13.5 m2 (0.6 x 1.05 ramp slot + eight wall bands 2.09 narrowing to 1.05 m over 8.0 m) x mean horizontal length 42.4 m | 570 (+/-40) | 36.2 |
| Queen's Chamber | 5.229 x 5.752 x (4.686 + 1.540/2) + niche about 3 | 167 | 23.9 |
| Queen's Chamber passage | 33.2 x 1.05 x 1.18 + 5.5 x 1.05 x 1.70 | 51 | 22.2 |
| Ascending passage | 39.3 x 1.266 less 4.2 m of plugs | 45 | 13.6 |
| Well shaft | 50 x 0.71 x 0.71 | 25 (+/-5) | about half above base |
| **Total to be filled** | | **about 860** | |

Swept volume of the whole descending passage: 105.2 x 1.266 = 133 m3. The part below base level: 30.0 / 0.4466 = 67.2 m, giving 85 m3.

- Strokes for everything: 860 / 133 = 6.5 (full length); 860 / 85 = 10.1 (bedrock part).
- Strokes for the gallery alone: 570 / 133 = 4.3; 570 / 85 = 6.7.
- Four strokes would need 215 m3 per stroke, i.e. a 170 m stroke at this cross-section.

Ideal lifting work, W = rho g sum(V x centroid height above source):

- From a reservoir at base level: gallery 570 x 9.81 x 36.2 = 202 MJ; Queen's Chamber 167 x 9.81 x 23.9 = 39 MJ; its passage 11 MJ; ascending passage 6 MJ; well shaft above base about 1 MJ. Total about 260 MJ (72 kWh).
- From chamber level (-30 m): add 845 x 9.81 x 30 = 249 MJ, total about 510 MJ.
- From the Old Kingdom water table (about -46 m): add 845 x 9.81 x 46 = 381 MJ, total about 640 MJ (178 kWh).
- "Per stroke" if four: 65 to 160 MJ. At a sustained 75 W per labourer (rule of thumb, not sourced) 260 MJ is about 960 person-hours with no losses. Energy is not the obstacle; force, sealing and water supply are.

Compressibility check: with a bulk modulus of about 2.2 GPa (textbook), 0.8 MPa shrinks water by 0.04 percent; "compressing" water stores negligible volume.

### 4.4 Suction limits

- Maximum suction lift: p_atm / (rho g) = 101.3 / 9.81 = 10.3 m, less vapour pressure (about 2.3 kPa at 20 C, about 0.24 m): about 10.1 m in theory, 6 to 8 m in practice.
- Lift demanded by C7: Old Kingdom water table (7 to 14 m asl) to chamber floor (28 to 30 m asl) = 14 to 23 m. Modern table: 11 to 15 m. Both exceed the limit before any piston height is added.
- Draining a sealed gallery: the gallery spans 21.66 to 51.5 m (29.8 m). If gas could not enter, any hanging water column taller than about 10.1 m would cavitate, so the largest suction the water can apply to the gas side is about 99 kPa. In practice the shafts admit air at tens of pascals (4.5), so the gallery stays at essentially atmospheric pressure and behaves as a slow displacement pump, not a vacuum pump.

### 4.5 Air flow through the King's Chamber shafts during draining

Shafts: A_N = 0.229 x 0.222 = 0.0507 m2, A_S = 0.216 x 0.224 = 0.0483 m2, hydraulic diameter about 0.22 m; L_N about 71 m, L_S about 53 m. Fully rough friction factor for 2 to 5 mm roughness: f about 0.04 to 0.05 (use 0.05). Loss coefficients: south 0.05 x 53/0.22 + 2.5 = 14.5; north 0.05 x 71/0.225 + 3.5 = 19.

Drain the gallery (570 m3) in one hour: Q = 0.158 m3/s. Splitting by conductance gives about 1.5 m/s in the north shaft and 1.7 m/s in the south; pressure drop = 19 x 1.2 x 1.5^2 / 2 = about 26 Pa (2.6 mm of water). Scaling with Q squared: a 10-minute drain needs about 0.9 kPa; a 6-hour drain under 1 Pa. Reynolds number about 2e4 (turbulent), Mach number under 0.01.

Antechamber grooves: four grooves about 2.8 in deep [S2]; width not recovered. If each is about 0.10 x 0.07 m (assumption), total 0.028 m2, velocity about 5.6 m/s for the one-hour drain and a loss of order 100 Pa. Still small. The limiting resistance is therefore whatever "seal" is assumed, not the shafts.

### 4.6 Leak-off (order of magnitude; inputs largely unknown)

Open joint, parallel-plate (cubic) law: flow per metre of joint = (rho g b^3 / 12 mu) x gradient = 8.2e5 x b^3 x i.

| aperture b | flow per metre of joint per unit gradient (m2/s) | whole gallery, about 1350 m of joint, gradient 15 | time to lose 570 m3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 mm (Petrie's casing mean, if open) | 1.0e-4 | about 2 m3/s | about 5 minutes |
| 0.1 mm | 8.2e-7 | about 17 L/s | about 9.5 hours |
| 0.05 mm | 1.0e-7 | about 2 L/s | about 3 days |

Assumptions: wall area about 800 m2 with blocks about 1 x 1.5 m (joint density 1.7 m per m2), mean head 15.5 m above local level, 1 m flow path. At 0.5 mm the joint Reynolds number is about 1500, so the cubic law overstates that row. The point is the cubic sensitivity: the outcome swings from "cannot be filled" to "holds for days" across a factor of ten in aperture, and gypsum mortar in the joints is water-soluble (about 2 g/L).

Matrix (Darcy) seepage: Q = K A i with A about 900 m2 and i about 15 gives 0.14 L/s at K = 1e-8 m/s and 14 L/s at K = 1e-6 m/s. K for this stone was not found.

Piston clearance: laminar slot flow through a 1 mm gap, perimeter 4.5 m, 1 m long, 0.75 MPa: Q = 4.5 x (1e-3)^3 x 7.5e5 / (12 x 1e-3 x 1) = 0.28 m3/s (an upper bound; the flow would be turbulent). Either way a millimetre of clearance defeats the stroke.

### 4.7 Hydraulic ram check (Cadman variant)

Assume the most favourable case: supply at base level, ram at chamber level (h = 30 m), delivery to the gallery roof (H = 81.5 m above the ram), efficiency 0.6 [S11]: q/Q = 0.6 x 30 / 81.5 = 0.22. To deliver 860 m3, about 3000 m3 must be spilled at chamber level. The chamber holds about 300 m3 and has no outfall, so it would flood and stall the ram within a fraction of one fill. Water-hammer scale (Joukowsky, textbook): pressure rise = rho x wave speed x velocity change, about 1 to 1.5 MPa per m/s of arrested flow.

## 5. What a simulation of this domain needs

### 5.1 Sub-problems and solver type

| sub-problem | physics | recommended solver type | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Regional water: can any water body exist at base level, and where is the water table under Khufu? | steady saturated groundwater flow with a Nile-stage boundary; perched-water sensitivity | 2D/3D Darcy groundwater model (finite difference or FEM, e.g. MODFLOW-class or OpenGeoSys-class) | gradients are small and slow; no free-surface CFD needed |
| B. Reservoir to passage to chamber to well shaft: filling, piston strokes, valve opening and closing, pressure transients | 1D unsteady pipe flow, storage nodes with level-volume curves, check valves, moving boundary (piston) | 1D hydraulic network: rigid-column for slow strokes, method of characteristics for water hammer and ram cycling | conduits are long and slender (L/D of 50 to 100); the questions are pressures, forces, volumes, timing |
| C. Filling the Queen's Chamber and Grand Gallery: free surface on a 26 deg slope, corbelled section, trapped gas at the roof, spill into the antechamber and King's Chamber | two-phase flow with a compressible gas phase | 3D volume-of-fluid CFD (interFoam / compressibleInterFoam class), geometry from the 3D model | gas trapping, overflow sill and interface area are three-dimensional and cannot be lumped credibly |
| D. Draining and the "syringe": gas drawn through shafts and grooves | gas network driven by the rate of change of water volume; later, buoyant mixing of dense SO2 with air | 1D duct network for flow rates and pressure; 3D buoyant multi-species CFD only for mixing in the antechamber/gallery (hand-off to the gas-chemistry domain) | section 4.5 shows the gas side is quasi-static; 3D matters only for composition |
| E. Leak-off through stone and joints | Darcy/Richards flow in matrix plus discrete fractures (cubic law), optional gypsum dissolution | variably saturated porous-media model with discrete fractures, or a lumped leak coefficient inside the 1D network for parameter sweeps | dominated by uncertain apertures; run as an ensemble |
| F. Piston seal and actuation | annular gap flow, friction, force balance | lumped ODE model coupled to B | no spatial resolution needed |
| G. Structural response to internal water pressure (hand-off) | masonry under 0.3 to 0.8 MPa internal pressure | FEM, outside this domain | needed to decide whether lining blocks stay seated |

Start with B, E (lumped) and F in one 1D model; it answers most pass/fail questions in seconds per run. Use C only for the gallery, antechamber and King's Chamber, after B shows a fill is possible at all.

### 5.2 Governing equations

- 1D conduits: continuity and momentum for unsteady pipe flow (water-hammer equations; rigid-column limit dV/dt = g (dH/L) - f V |V| / 2D); Darcy-Weisbach friction with Colebrook roughness; minor losses at junctions.
- Storage nodes: dV/dt = sum of inflows, with z(V) from the 3D geometry.
- Check valves: flow only for positive head difference, plus a cracking pressure and a closing-time parameter.
- Piston: m x'' = driving force - p A - friction - weight component; leakage Q = C b^3 dp / (mu L).
- VOF: incompressible Navier-Stokes for water, compressible ideal gas for the gas phase, interface advection, k-omega SST turbulence, surface tension negligible at this scale.
- Porous/fracture: Darcy q = -K grad h; Richards equation for the unsaturated masonry outside the lining; fracture transmissivity rho g b^3 / 12 mu.
- Gas network: Darcy-Weisbach with air properties; ideal-gas storage in the King's Chamber and gallery headspace.

### 5.4 Key dimensionless numbers

- Reynolds number in conduits (water): about 1e5 for 0.1 m3/s in the well shaft; in shafts (air): about 2e4.
- Froude number at the gallery free surface (bore formation during filling on a 26 deg slope).
- Cavitation number, (p - p_vapour) / (0.5 rho v^2), on the suction stroke and on draining.
- Suction ratio: required lift / 10.1 m. Must be below 1 (practically below 0.7).
- Stroke-volume ratio: total fill volume / (strokes x swept volume). Must be 1 or less.
- Leak ratio: leak-off rate / pump delivery rate. Must be well below 1 for a fill to complete.
- Valve-closure ratio: closure time / (2L / wave speed), which decides whether ram-type pressure pulses form.
- Fracture Reynolds number, to check the cubic law.
- Mach number for the gas side (far below 0.3, so the incompressible treatment is adequate except for the trapped pocket).

### 5.5 Pass/fail criteria (neutral; each can go either way)

1. **Source test.** With the evidence-based boundary (no base-level reservoir), does any water reach the Subterranean Chamber? Pass requires an explicitly assumed reservoir; report what level and volume it must have and that this is an assumption.
2. **Suction test.** With the water table at the measured or reconstructed level, does a retracting piston draw water through the pit without the pressure anywhere falling to vapour pressure? Fail if required lift exceeds about 10 m.
3. **Volume test.** Does the stated number of strokes (four) raise the water surface to the gallery roof, given the real swept volume? Report the number of strokes actually needed.
4. **Force test.** Peak piston force and chamber pressure; pass only if a named actuator and seal can supply 0.6 to 1 MN and hold 0.5 to 0.8 MPa.
5. **Containment test.** Find the joint aperture and matrix conductivity below which the gallery holds, say, 90 percent of its water for the stated process time. Compare with measured apertures when available. The hypothesis passes only if the required tightness is within what the masonry shows.
6. **Gas-lock test (VOF).** With the gallery filled, does water stay out of the King's Chamber while gas can still pass the antechamber? Report the trapped gas volume and the King's Chamber water depth.
7. **Syringe test.** For a stated drain rate, how much of the replacement gas comes down the shafts versus other leaks, and what is the pressure difference? Pass if most of the gas comes via the intended path for realistic leak areas.
8. **Ram test (Cadman).** Does a ram cycle sustain itself with the chamber as it exists (no outfall)? Report cycles before stall and the delivered head.

A result favourable to the hypothesis would be: tests 2 to 7 passing with boundary values inside the evidence-based ranges of section 5.3. A result unfavourable would be: any of tests 1 to 3 failing at every point in those ranges.

- Strokes: 860 / 133 = 6.5; 860 / 85 = 10.1; gallery alone 570 / 133 = 4.3 (585 / 133 = 4.4). Four strokes deliver 533 m3 (full length) or 340 m3 (below base).
- Work from a base-level reservoir: 9.81 x (570 x 36.2 + 167 x 23.9 + 51 x 22.2 + 45 x 13.6 + 12 x 10.8) = 260 MJ (72 kWh). Adding the lift of 845 m3 from -30 m: 509 MJ; from -46 m: 641 MJ; from -43 m: 616 MJ.

**W4. Cross-check of base elevation and flood level.** Perring's 1838 high Nile is 41.8 m below the base, i.e. about 18.2 m asl if the base is 60. Lehner's flood peak of 13.5 to 14 m plus his 4 to 5 m of later sediment gives 17.5 to 19 m. The two agree, which supports both the 60 m base and Lehner's lower flood level; it sits less comfortably with a 17 m Old Kingdom flood. This is my own consistency check, not a published one.

### Steelman: the strongest published material in favour, and what it does and does not show

1. **Perched water does occur on the plateau today.** Geophysics interprets perched water at about 22 to 48 m elevation on the higher ground toward Menkaure, tens of metres above the regional table [S4]. This shows the rock sequence can hold water above the regional level. It is modern, attributed to runoff and urban leakage, not drilled, and nothing of the kind is reported at Khufu. A perched body above about 20 m asl under Khufu in the Old Kingdom would overturn C7; none is documented.
2. **A higher Old Kingdom flood.** Younes et al. 2024 put the flood about 3 m higher than Lehner [S18]. That is the most favourable published level; it still leaves the water about 43 m below the base.
3. **Rain reaches the pit.** Petrie saw water in the pit after heavy rain ran down the entrance passage [S2], and the Giza floodplain cores record wadi outwash from torrential rain on the plateau (dated 1150 to 900 BCE) [S18]. Runoff on the plateau is real; the documented drainage runs south-east toward the central wadi, away from Khufu [S3][S18].
4. **Hydraulic works at other pyramids are now argued in peer-reviewed journals.** Landreau et al. 2024 (Saqqara) [S14] and Landreau, Piton and Hemeda 2026 (two possible dams at Dahshur, crest near 60 m asl, reservoir about 230,000 m3, catchment 3.2 km2) [S20]. Both rest on remote sensing and re-reading of known structures; neither has been excavated or dated, and the Saqqara paper has drawn published criticism (a critique and an authors' reply in Ancient Egypt magazine were seen in search results only and were not opened). Drumm's statement that reservoir-fed shafts exist at the Step and Red pyramids therefore has a published, though unconfirmed, counterpart for the reservoir part only.
5. **Does the Saqqara argument transfer to Giza?** No, for specific reasons. It depends on (a) a wadi catchment upslope of the monument (15 km2, perhaps 400 km2 by an unconfirmed diversion), (b) a dam whose outlet (48.7 m asl) is at or above the level of the works, (c) a rock-cut trench holding water at 40 to 52 m asl beside the pyramid, and (d) a 7 m square, 28 m deep shaft that fills by gravity under 10 to 20 m of head. At Khufu there is no reported dam, moat or trench; the natural drainage leads away from the pyramid; the only known entry is 17 m above the pavement; and the hypothesis needs water raised 51.5 m above the base through passages about 1.05 x 1.2 m, not let in by gravity. What transfers is only the general proposition that Old Kingdom builders could impound runoff, which the 14 m high Sadd el-Kafara dam already shows [S3]. The authors themselves raise other pyramids only as a question.
6. **Water budget alone does not exclude a rain-fed supply.** With the rainfall and runoff ranges used in [S14] (50 to 150 mm per year at 1 to 3 percent; storms of 50 mm at up to 30 percent), one square kilometre yields 500 to 4500 m3 per year, or 15,000 m3 in one storm, against about 860 m3 per gallery fill. This is my arithmetic, it assumes a catchment and a basin for which there is no evidence at Khufu, and it says nothing about holding the water on karstified limestone.
7. **The stone may be tighter than its porosity suggests.** See C12. This favours the hypothesis on matrix seepage and leaves joints as the deciding unknown.
8. **Cadman.** Nothing quantitative could be added in his favour: no primary write-up was reachable, and the second-hand account has no pressures, flows, heads or scale.

Not found, after searching specifically for it: any published report of an Old Kingdom canal, basin, cistern, lining, lacustrine sediment or rain-harvesting work at or above the level of Khufu's pavement.
